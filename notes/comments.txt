## 1. Obvious counterarguments you should anticipate

### A. "Your setting is too toy / too narrow"

**Counterargument:**  
A 4.6M word-level GPT on 100k child-directed tokens is too small and specialized; results may not generalize to modern LLMs or to typical NLP/ML datasets.

**How to respond/prepare:**

- Make the *goal* explicit: this is a *minimalist, mechanistic* study optimized for interpretability, not benchmark state-of-the-art.
- Emphasize that small-data, small-model regimes are exactly where curriculum and restarts are most contentious and where inductive biases actually matter.
- If possible, add at least one "non-child-language" replication (e.g., a tiny news or Wikipedia subset) or a non-language modality (simple vision classification) to show the substitutes/priors pattern isn't idiosyncratic to BabyLM.

---

### B. "Curriculum vs. prior isn't really 'substitutes'; it's flawed design/implementation"

**Counterargument:**  
The fact that the curriculum hurts with the structured prior might be an artifact:

- the curriculum definition interacts badly with your prior,
- the prior is mis-specified,
- the learning rate schedule or optimizer is tuned for one condition and not the other.

**How to respond/prepare:**

- Show robustness across:
  - Different curriculum orderings (short->long, easy-lexical->complex-syntactic, etc.).
  - Variants of the structured prior (e.g., coarser vs. finer categories).
- Check and report that hyperparameters (optimizer, LR, batch size) are held constant, or do a small tuning sweep for both conditions.
- Clarify that "substitutes" is about *effective regularisation strength*: when both curriculum and prior constrain the model early, total regularisation crosses a sweet spot.

---

### C. "Curriculum *can* be a complement with the right design"

**Counterargument:**  
There are theoretical and empirical works where curriculum and shaping/priors *do* cooperate. Your negative result might depend on using a simplistic difficulty notion.

**How to respond/prepare:**

- Explicitly carve out your claim: in *this* low-data, small-model setting, with *this type* of structured prior, curriculum behaves as a substitute.
- Point to conditions under which complementarity is plausible:
  - curricula that target aspects orthogonal to the prior (e.g., semantic difficulty vs. syntactic structure),
  - curricula that are adaptive rather than static.
- If possible, include a "positive" setup where curriculum and a different prior *do* work together, or show that your prior and curriculum are strongly overlapping in what they regularize (justifying the "double-counting" metaphor).

---

### D. "Warm restarts already have a theory; you're rediscovering standard practice"

**Counterargument:**  
There's prior work showing that restarts and cyclical schedules are tied to escaping local minima, exploring new regions, and interacting with non-stationary tasks (e.g., continual learning). Your "data change alignment" story might be incremental.

**How to respond/prepare:**

- Acknowledge existing accounts (e.g., SGDR / cosine restarts, cyclical LR, continual learning schedules) and say clearly what you add:
  - an *empirical control* that shows restarts hurt when misaligned with actual data regime changes;
  - a clear experimental contrast: same schedule, different data ordering, opposite effect.
- Frame your contribution as a *clarifying test*: "Here is a simple way to falsify 'warm restarts as free trick' by comparing aligned vs. misaligned restarts."

---

### E. "Seed variance / low-data fragility is well known"

**Counterargument:**  
Others have already argued that variance over random seeds can be large, and that multi-seed reporting is necessary. Your point may be seen as preaching to the choir.

**How to respond/prepare:**

- Cite prior work that documents seed variance (there are papers on reproducibility and instability in deep learning, especially on small datasets and in RL).
- Emphasize *how* your case study demonstrates this:
  - one condition flipping from "best" to "worst" when you move from 1 to 3 seeds;
  - effect sizes comparable to typical claimed improvements.
- Position your message as: "Here is a concrete, low-data scenario illustrating why the guidelines matter, not just abstract advice."

---

### F. "Bits-per-character and child data aren't mainstream metrics"

**Counterargument:**  
BPC on child-directed text is not a standard evaluation; others might prefer perplexity on more canonical corpora.

**How to respond/prepare:**

- Explain briefly why BPC is sensible here (continuous measure, sensitive to small changes; common in character-level LM).
- If feasible, add a more standard metric (perplexity, downstream simple task) on the same setup.
- Or explicitly claim: "We chose BPC in this regime for sensitivity, and we treat this as a mechanistic rather than benchmark-oriented study."

Related Work
Curriculum learning was introduced as a way to improve optimization and generalization by ordering training examples from easy to hard, most notably in early work on neural networks and self-paced learning [e.g., Bengio et al., 2009; Kumar et al., 2010]. Subsequent studies in NLP and sequence modeling have applied curricula to language modeling and other tasks, often reporting modest or mixed gains depending on architecture and data regime. Our results speak directly to this apparent inconsistency: we show that in a low-data setting, curricula function as a form of regularisation that helps models with weak inductive bias but can interfere with models that already embody strong structured priors.

There is also a line of work on injecting explicit structure into neural language models, for example via grammar-aware embeddings, POS-informed representations, or hybrid architectures that combine neural LMs with symbolic or typed components. These approaches treat inductive bias as an explicit design parameter rather than an emergent property of training data alone. Our “substitutes, not complements” finding connects this literature to curriculum learning, demonstrating that a curriculum can partially duplicate the role of a structured prior instead of simply augmenting it.

Learning-rate schedules and warm restarts have been extensively studied as generic optimization tools. Cosine-annealing schedules with periodic restarts and cyclical learning rates [e.g., Loshchilov & Hutter, 2017; Smith, 2017] are typically motivated in terms of exploration of new minima or improved convergence, largely independent of assumptions about the training data distribution. In contrast, we frame learning-rate restarts as a hypothesis about the data rather than only about the optimizer: in our experiments, restarts help precisely when they coincide with genuine regime changes in the training stream and hurt when the same schedule is applied to a stationary data order.

Finally, our methodological emphasis on multi-seed evaluation aligns with broader concerns about variance and reproducibility in deep learning, particularly in small-data and reinforcement learning settings where performance can vary substantially across random initializations and data shuffles. Prior work has argued that single-seed reports are insufficient in such regimes; our case study in low-data language modeling quantifies how seed-to-seed variability can invert apparent “best” conditions and reinforces the need to report performance bands rather than single-point estimates.
