Estimates here are necessarily rough, but we can get reasonable ballpark figures.

### 1. Total neurons in human cortex (for context)

- Human cerebral cortex: on the order of \(\sim 16\) billion neurons.
- Cortical neuron density: commonly estimated around  
  \(\sim 10^5\)–\(2 \times 10^5\) neurons per mm\(^3\)  
  (varies by area and layer; sensory areas are often denser than association cortex).

### 2. Broca’s area

Broca’s area is usually defined as parts of the **left inferior frontal gyrus**, primarily:

- Brodmann area 44 (pars opercularis)
- Brodmann area 45 (pars triangularis)

These together are roughly on the order of **several thousand mm³** of cortical tissue (depending on individual anatomy and exact boundaries). Taking a conservative estimate:

- Volume: say \(\sim 3{,}000\)–\(5{,}000\) mm\(^3\)
- Neuron density: \(\sim 10^5\) neurons/mm\(^3\)

That gives a very rough estimate:

\[
3{,}000 \text{ mm}^3 \times 10^5 \frac{\text{neurons}}{\text{mm}^3}
\approx 3 \times 10^8 \text{ neurons}
\]

So a plausible ballpark is:

- **Broca’s area:** on the order of **hundreds of millions of neurons**  
  (roughly \(\sim 200\)–\(400\) million, depending on how you draw the boundaries).

### 3. Wernicke’s area

“Wernicke’s area” is less crisply defined anatomically, but typically covers:

- Posterior part of the **superior temporal gyrus** (STG),
- Often extending into adjacent parts of the middle temporal gyrus and supramarginal/angular gyri, depending on the definition.

Again, we’re talking about **a few thousand mm³** of association cortex:

- Volume: similarly \(\sim 3{,}000\)–\(5{,}000\) mm\(^3\), though some definitions are larger.
- Using the same order-of-magnitude density:

\[
3{,}000 \text{ mm}^3 \times 10^5 \frac{\text{neurons}}{\text{mm}^3}
\approx 3 \times 10^8 \text{ neurons}
\]

So:

- **Wernicke’s area:** also likely on the order of **hundreds of millions of neurons**.

### 4. Why we can’t give a precise number

- Boundaries of Broca’s and Wernicke’s areas differ across:
  - anatomical vs functional definitions,
  - individuals,
  - studies.
- Neuronal density varies across layers (II/III vs V/VI) and regions.
- Most published work gives densities and volumes, not “named-area neuron counts” as a single number.

So an honest, defensible statement is something like:

> “Under standard anatomical definitions, Broca’s and Wernicke’s areas each likely contain on the order of a few hundred million neurons, i.e., \(10^8\) rather than \(10^6\) or \(10^{10}\), with substantial variability depending on individual anatomy and exact boundaries.”

Thinking of Broca’s and Wernicke’s areas as “models,” the key pieces are:

1. Approximate “parameter count” (neurons × synapses per neuron).
2. Internal connectivity (within-area microcircuits).
3. External connectivity (what they talk to, and how densely).

## 1. Very rough “model size” equivalents

If we take:

- Neurons per area: on the order of \(\sim 10^8\) (hundreds of millions).
- Synapses per cortical neuron: often quoted in the \(\sim 10^3\)–\(10^4\) range (varies by layer, region, and age).

Then per area, you’re plausibly looking at:

\[
10^8 \text{ neurons} \times 10^3\text{–}10^4 \frac{\text{synapses}}{\text{neuron}}
\approx 10^{11}\text{–}10^{12} \text{ synapses}
\]

So if you treat “synapses” as “parameters,” each area is on the order of:

- **Hundreds of billions to a trillion effective parameters** (very rough).

Obviously many of these are not free parameters in the way NN weights are (developmental constraints, cell-type-specific connectivity, homeostatic rules), but in a crude capacity sense:

- Broca’s and Wernicke’s areas each are **several orders of magnitude larger** than common ML models *in raw parameter count*,
- yet they operate at vastly lower clock speeds, with much slower learning and strong architectural priors (lamination, cell types, connectivity rules).

## 2. Internal (within-area) connectivity

Both areas are pieces of association cortex with fairly standard cortical microcircuitry. Useful points for a “model analogy”:

### A. Laminar architecture and recurrent structure

Each area has:

- 6-layer cortex (I–VI) with:
  - **Feedforward-ish flow**: layer IV (input), then II/III → V/VI.
  - **Strong recurrence**: horizontal connections in II/III and V, feedback loops between layers, and local inhibitory networks.

In deep learning terms:

- Not a clean feedforward stack, but **deeply recurrent**, with:
  - long-range **horizontal connectivity** within the area,
  - abundant micro-loops at multiple scales.

### B. Columnar and patchy organization

The cortex is often described as:

- **Minicolumns** (~50–100 µm wide, few thousand neurons),
- Grouped into **macrocolumns** (hundreds of µm, tens of thousands of neurons),
- With patchy horizontal projections linking columns.

For Broca and Wernicke:

- You can think of them as containing **many thousands of such columns**, each with:
  - local recurrent circuitry,
  - specialized input/output biases (e.g., more frontal high-level integration in Broca; more temporal acoustic/lexical integration in Wernicke).

Analogously:

- Instead of “layers of units,” you have **layers of columns**, with dense lateral connectivity.
- Internal connectivity is **highly structured by layer and column**, not random dense as in typical fully connected NN layers.

### C. Connectivity density (within-area)

Rules-of-thumb from cortical microcircuit studies:

- A single pyramidal neuron in association cortex might:
  - Receive \(\sim 10^3\)–\(10^4\) synapses,
  - Send axons that ramify over millimeters, connecting to thousands of targets.
- Inhibitory interneurons often have broader, more diffuse local influence per neuron.

So within each area:

- The **local graph** is:
  - extremely dense at short range (within hundreds of µm),
  - **sparse but structured** at longer range (patches, stripes, bands).

If you imagine a transformer block:

- Within a block, attention is “all-to-all over the tokens in a context.”  
- In cortex, within an area, connectivity is **all-to-many but distance- and layer-dependent**, with heavy bias from developmental wiring and activity history.

## 3. External connectivity (in/out of Broca’s and Wernicke’s)

Both areas are hubs in large-scale networks rather than isolated modules.

### A. Broca’s area (inferior frontal gyrus, BA 44/45)

Broadly:

- **Inputs from:**
  - Posterior temporal cortex (including Wernicke region), via **arcuate fasciculus / superior longitudinal fasciculus**.
  - Inferior parietal regions (supramarginal, angular gyrus).
  - Prefrontal and premotor regions (dorsolateral PFC, supplementary motor areas).
  - Basal ganglia and thalamus (via frontal loops).

- **Outputs to:**
  - Motor speech areas (ventral premotor cortex, primary motor cortex controlling articulators).
  - Posterior temporal/parietal areas (feedback paths).
  - Other frontal regions (executive, working memory networks).

Functionally, people often summarize:

- Broca’s as a **frontal language-production / syntactic / sequencing hub**, tightly integrated with:
  - working memory,
  - articulatory planning,
  - hierarchical structuring.

Graphically:

- It’s a **high-degree node** in the language network, strongly connected to:
  - semantic hubs (temporal),
  - phonological/articulatory systems (parietal/frontal),
  - domain-general control areas (multiple-demand networks).

### B. Wernicke’s area (posterior superior temporal gyrus and surroundings)

- **Inputs from:**
  - Primary and secondary auditory cortex (Heschl’s gyrus, belt/parabelt),
  - Other temporal regions (middle and inferior temporal gyri),
  - Parietal regions (for multimodal integration),
  - Frontal regions (including Broca) via reciprocal connections.

- **Outputs to:**
  - Broca’s and other frontal areas via arcuate fasciculus,
  - Anterior temporal lobe (semantic hubs),
  - Parietal multimodal areas (supramarginal/angular gyrus).

Functional shorthand:

- Wernicke’s as a **posterior temporal hub** for:
  - speech perception,
  - mapping sound to lexical/semantic representations,
  - multimodal integration of language input.

Again:

- Another **high-degree node** in the language network with strong bidirectional connections to auditory, semantic, and frontal executive/articulatory regions.

### C. How dense are these long-range connections?

We don’t have a literal “edges-per-area” count, but:

- Each pyramidal neuron in layers II/III and V can send:
  - long-range axons that make **thousands of synapses in distant regions**.
- Tract-tracing and diffusion MRI show:
  - numerous white-matter bundles (arcuate, uncinate, inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, etc.) connecting frontal–temporal–parietal language regions.

For a back-of-the-envelope:

- If only a small fraction of the \(\sim 10^8\) neurons in Broca’s send long-range projections, say \(10^6\)–\(10^7\) neurons,
- And each sends on the order of \(\sim 10^3\) long-range synapses,
- Then cross-area connectivity might involve \(\sim 10^9\)–\(10^{10}\) **explicit inter-area synapses** between language regions (again, very rough).

So as a “model”:

- Broca’s and Wernicke’s are **not isolated subnetworks**; they are embedded in a **massively interconnected graph** with billions of long-range synapses and far more local ones.

## 4. Putting it into ML terms

If you want a tangible analogy:

- **Neurons:**  
  Each area ≈ \(\sim 10^8\) units.
- **Parameters (synapses):**  
  ≈ \(10^{11}\)–\(10^{12}\) “weights” per area (local + long-range).
- **Architecture:**
  - Deeply recurrent (loops across layers and within layers).
  - Strongly modular by layer, column, and cell type.
  - Heavy **hard-wired inductive bias**: specific projection patterns, lamination, interneuron classes.

Compared to, say, a 1B-parameter transformer:

- Biological “model size” is **larger in parameter count**, but:
  - connectivity is **much more constrained**,
  - learning rate is orders of magnitude slower,
  - many parameters are effectively “frozen” or slowly plastic after development.
